# Project Report: Quantum VAE for Neuroimaging

## 1. Overview

This research project investigates a **Quantum Variational Autoencoder (QVAE)** for **depression detection from resting-state fMRI**, and compares it against a **classical PCA + SVM baseline**.

**Stack:** PennyLane, cuQuantum on NVIDIA GPUs, fMRI data.

## 2. Problem Statement

Resting-state fMRI produces very high-dimensional data, while labelled clinical datasets are small. That combination is hard for learning methods: there are many features and few subjects, so models overfit easily and compact, informative representations matter a great deal.

The research question: **can a quantum variational autoencoder learn a useful compressed representation of fMRI features for detecting depression, and how does it compare with a classical pipeline?**

## 3. Approach

### Classical Baseline: PCA + SVM

The baseline reduces dimensionality with **PCA** and classifies with an **SVM**. It is simple, well understood and strong on small data, which makes it the right bar to compare against. A new method that cannot beat it is not yet justified.

### Quantum VAE

A variational autoencoder learns to compress inputs into a small latent space and reconstruct them. In the quantum version, a **parameterized quantum circuit** plays the role of the encoder/decoder, with its parameters trained classically through a hybrid quantum-classical loop. The learned latent representation then feeds the downstream depression classification.

### Tooling

- **PennyLane** defines the quantum circuits and provides differentiation for training.
- **cuQuantum** on **NVIDIA GPUs** accelerates the quantum circuit simulation, which would otherwise be a bottleneck for iterating on circuit designs.

## 4. Evaluation Plan

The central design principle is a **fair comparison**: same data, same splits, same evaluation metrics for both the QVAE pipeline and the PCA+SVM baseline. With small clinical datasets, careful cross-validation is essential, and subject-level splits are needed so that data from one participant never appears in both training and test sets.

## 5. Challenges and Design Considerations

- **Simulation cost.** Simulating qubits scales exponentially, so circuit width is limited; GPU acceleration extends what is practical.
- **Data size.** Small cohorts raise variance, so conclusions should be stated carefully.
- **Input encoding.** Getting high-dimensional fMRI features into a small number of qubits requires deliberate dimensionality reduction and encoding choices.
- **Optimization.** Variational circuits can be hard to train (for example, flat loss landscapes), so initialization and circuit depth matter.

## 6. Status and Next Steps

This is ongoing research. Next steps include completing the benchmark against the classical baseline, ablating circuit design choices, and testing robustness across datasets.
